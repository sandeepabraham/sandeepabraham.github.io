The landscape

Regulatory blind spots
aren't accidents.
They're being
weaponized.

A Chinese crime syndicate registered shell companies in Colorado for $50, fraudulently enrolled as a SEC investment adviser with a nonexistent officer, and used those credentials to launder tens of millions from U.S. victims. Every step exploited a known, documented regulatory gap — from the Treasury's suspension of the Corporate Transparency Act to the SEC's lack of identity verification for registered advisers. The regulators knew. They chose not to fix it.

This is the defining pattern of digital harm right now: adversaries don't need to outmaneuver defenses. They read the policy documents, find where enforcement stops, and build their operations in that exact space. AI-generated deepfakes, WhatsApp social engineering, cross-jurisdictional shell networks, fraudulent regulatory registrations — none of this is technically sophisticated. It's institutionally sophisticated. It's regulatory arbitrage at industrial scale.

The same playbooks running in crypto fraud are running against AI platforms, election information ecosystems, and the legal systems that are supposed to provide redress. The threat actors have generalized. The defenses mostly haven't.

Most advisors understand policy or engineering. I understand the adversary. My work bridges the gap between what regulatory frameworks say and what sophisticated actors actually do inside those gaps — because I've spent months living inside those operations.
